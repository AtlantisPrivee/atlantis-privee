Medical Malpractice Questions: Records, Standard of Care, and Deadlines

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 June 15, 2026 ⏱ 3 min read

An unexpected outcome can raise questions about what happened and whether the care met the applicable professional standard. The injury, the care at issue, and the connection between them need separate evidence. Florida, for example, defines a prevailing professional standard of care in its medical negligence statute. California's courts describe the need to prove both inadequate care and that it caused harm. Florida Statutes § 766.102 · California Courts medical malpractice guide

Identify the question a record can answer

  • Diagnosis and follow-up. Compare when symptoms were reported, when a test was ordered, when the result was available, and what was communicated afterward.
  • Procedure or medication. Identify the procedure or medication, date, order, and administration record. Compare the instructions given before and after the visit.
  • Consent. Preserve the consent form and records about what was discussed. A signature and the actual conversation can address different questions.
  • Harm and later care. Record the symptoms, follow-up evaluations, and treatment the later clinicians documented. Keep later conclusions separate from the original visit record.

Request the relevant records from the appropriate covered provider or plan. HHS explains that the right of access covers much health and billing information in designated record sets, subject to exceptions. Ask which document and date range the provider can supply. The Medical Records Tracker can help identify missing visits or reports. HHS guidance on access to health information

Build a chronology before assessing causation

For example, a delayed-result question can have four dates: the test order, the lab result, the clinician's documented review, and the patient's follow-up. A result date alone may leave the communication question open. Keep original copies and the source of any date you add. A qualified reviewer can assess the care and what would likely have happened under different circumstances. California Courts medical malpractice guide

Check for procedures before filing

Special rules can apply. Florida's medical negligence law provides for presuit investigation and notice, including a corroborating expert opinion under the statute's terms. California's courts explain its own timing and notice rules. These are state examples. Verify the applicable dates and procedures early, especially if a public healthcare entity may be involved. Florida Statutes §§ 766.106 and 766.203 · California Courts medical malpractice guide

Use the filing deadline guide to separate notice, lawsuit filing, and other clocks. Bring your chronology, actual records, and open questions to a first consultation. For ongoing symptoms or treatment decisions, contact an appropriate healthcare professional.
