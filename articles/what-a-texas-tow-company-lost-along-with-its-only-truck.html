What a Texas Tow Company Lost Along With Its Only Truck

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

When J&D Towing lost its only tow truck, its balance sheet took two hits. A Dodge 3500 disappeared from the business. So did the ability to take a call and send a truck. A check for the value of the Dodge could pay for a replacement. It could do little about the jobs the company had already missed.

The Texas Supreme Court used that distinction to decide J&D Towing, LLC v. American Alternative Insurance Corporation. In 2016 it held that an owner of totally destroyed personal property can recover proven loss-of-use damages in addition to the property's fair market value under Texas law. The case illustrates the work that goes into proving the second loss. Texas Supreme Court opinion, pp. 2–5, 28–32

The $25,000 payment covered two different things

The December 2011 collision destroyed the Huntsville company's only tow truck. The other driver's insurer eventually paid its $25,000 property damage limit. J&D used the money to buy another truck and resumed business in March 2012. It pursued the uncovered loss of use under its own underinsured motorist policy. That policy tied payment to damages the insured was legally entitled to recover.

The jury found $28,000 in loss-of-use damages. The trial court valued the truck at $19,500, leaving $5,500 of the prior $25,000 payment attributable to loss of use. It credited that $5,500 against the jury's loss-of-use award. The insurer disputed whether Texas law allowed loss of use after total destruction. The state Supreme Court resolved that legal issue in J&D's favor and rendered judgment for the company. J&D Towing, pp. 3–5 and 31–32

The arithmetic answers a useful question. Once part of a previous payment covered downtime, how much downtime remained uncompensated in the trial court's calculation? Here the difference was $22,500. The policy, evidence, and credit drove that result. A property damage payment in another case may have a different allocation.

Proving downtime takes a different file from proving value

Comparable truck listings can support a pre-loss value. The downtime file needs evidence about what the business was equipped to earn, which expenses it avoided, and how long replacement reasonably took. Keep the two files connected by the same dates and vehicle identifier.

For a business vehicle, dispatch logs can establish how many eligible jobs arrived on particular dates. Invoices, payments, and prior work records can show the usual income from that kind of job. Fuel and trip expenses can show what the business would have spent to do the work. A rental inquiry may show whether a substitute was available and suitable for the work.

The opinion describes measures that can fit different facts, including lost profits and the reasonable rental cost or value of a substitute. It also limits loss-of-use recovery to damage that can be reasonably tied to the loss and to a reasonable period for replacement, with the usual requirements of proof. A calendar that runs through every day until a check arrives can hide the difference between an unavoidable delay and time a workable substitute was available. J&D Towing, discussion of loss-of-use limits

Count a missed job at its contribution

Take a hypothetical week in which a truck normally completes 12 tows at $150 each. Each completed job requires $35 in incremental fuel, dispatch, and other trip costs. Suppose the business can complete four of those jobs with another truck. The eight remaining jobs represent $1,200 in forgone sales and $280 in avoided trip costs, for $920 in estimated lost contribution in this simplified example.

Then test the assumption. Were those eight actual requests or a historical average? Could another operator have handled more of them? Does the $35 include any costs the business had to pay even while the truck sat? The answers can change the calculation materially. Keep recurring fixed costs separate and document how any profit estimate treats them.

If a rented truck covered a job, use the revenue and rental records for the same period. Counting the entire job as lost and including the expense of the substitute that performed it would count incompatible outcomes for that job.

Give the replacement timeline a beginning and an end

Use the collision date as an anchor. Record when the total loss was confirmed, what substitutes were available, which listings you checked, what made a replacement suitable, and when the business actually resumed. A vehicle that requires special equipment or inspection may take more evidence to replace than a readily available passenger car.

Organize the claim as three reconciled amounts. The first is the value of the property. The second is the supported cost of lost use or of a substitute for the relevant interval. The third is what has already been paid for either loss. That last column explained the $5,500 credit in J&D Towing.
