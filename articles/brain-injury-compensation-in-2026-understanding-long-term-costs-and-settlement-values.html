Brain Injury Claims: Care, Work, and Daily Function

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 June 17, 2026 ⏱ 3 min read

Traumatic brain injury can affect memory, concentration, balance, sleep, mood, and other functions. After a head injury, get medical guidance about new symptoms. CDC lists danger signs requiring immediate emergency care, including repeated vomiting, seizures, or a headache that worsens and does not go away. Symptoms can emerge later. The clinical record and follow-up matter for care. CDC concussion symptoms and danger signs

Record change over time using specific tasks

Start a timeline with the injury, first evaluation, follow-up visits, and documented changes in symptoms or function. Use the provider's wording for diagnoses and restrictions. For observations at home, write down the task, what happened, and whether assistance or extra time was needed. Someone who previously managed a weekly grocery trip alone, for example, might now use a list, require a ride, or need a break partway through. The Daily Activity Recovery Journal can organize comparable entries.

Rehabilitation can address different needs. Speech-language, occupational, physical, or other therapies may be recommended depending on the injury. Record who recommended each service, the treatment goal, and the date of reassessment. NINDS traumatic brain injury overview

Connect work restrictions to actual earnings and tasks

A return-to-work plan may describe shorter shifts, rest breaks, or changes in duties. Compare the written plan with actual hours and pay across the same dates. A dated work schedule and pay statement can help show when the change affected earnings. The Return-to-Work Hours and Pay Calculator compares baseline and actual hours under an entered rate. A claim about future capacity needs a separate assessment. CDC guidance on returning to activities after a mild TBI · Lost wages documentation guide

Keep the care estimate separate from the injury narrative

A service log can show what rehabilitation took place. A budget can show what an expected future course might cost. The two should use different source columns. For care already received, verify itemized charges against the plan's explanation and actual payments. For care that may be needed, use the provider's recommendation and quote or rate source. CMS explanation of benefits guide

The long-term care cost guide shows how to document frequency, price, duration, and replacement assumptions. If the same difficulty is relevant to daily life as well as work, keep those effects distinct. For example, reduced paid work hours belong in a wage record, while the need for help with meals belongs in a daily-function or support record.
