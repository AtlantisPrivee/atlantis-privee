Why Ford Could Be Sued Where the Crash Happened

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

A Ford Explorer first sold in Washington ended up in Montana. A Ford Crown Victoria first sold in North Dakota ended up in Minnesota. Both vehicles were involved in serious crashes, and both lawsuits raised a question about their history. Could the courts in the states where the crashes happened hear claims against Ford when Ford had first sold those particular cars elsewhere? U.S. Supreme Court opinion in Ford

The U.S. Supreme Court said yes on the facts of these two cases. In its March 25, 2021 decision in Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, it considered Ford's business in each state and how that business related to the claims. The first-sale receipt mattered to Ford's argument. The rest of the record explained why that receipt did not settle the jurisdiction question. Supreme Court opinion, 592 U.S. 351, slip opinion pp. 1–4, 8–14

Two vehicles and two routes into court

The Montana case involved a 1996 Explorer. A rear tire's tread separated, the vehicle rolled, and the driver died. Her estate sued in Montana, alleging a design defect, a failure to warn, and negligence. In Minnesota, a 1994 Crown Victoria hit a snowplow. A passenger was seriously injured when the airbag did not deploy. He brought claims in Minnesota over the vehicle and the collision. U.S. Supreme Court opinion in Ford

Ford had designed and manufactured both vehicles outside the two states where the lawsuits were filed. It argued that its contacts with each state did not cause the injury because the particular vehicles had arrived there through resale and relocation. The plaintiffs also had evidence about Ford's ongoing efforts to sell, advertise, and service the same models in the states where the respective crashes occurred. Ford, slip opinion pp. 2–5

Why the market for the model mattered

Personal jurisdiction is a court's authority over a defendant. The question here was specific jurisdiction, which links that authority to the claims in the particular lawsuit. The Court looked for a relationship between Ford's activities in each state and the dispute there. Ford served the in-state market for the models, the victims were residents, and the crashes and injuries occurred in those states. U.S. Supreme Court opinion in Ford

The Court rejected the need for a strict causal chain from Ford's in-state business to the initial sale of the particular vehicle in these circumstances. The majority emphasized that the relationship still has limits. The case supports looking beyond a vehicle's first sale when there is a meaningful connection between the defendant's forum activities and the dispute. Ford, slip opinion pp. 6–15

Jurisdiction clears a threshold to litigating the claims. The proof of a defect, the connection between the alleged defect and the injury, and the amount of any damages are questions for the merits. Keep evidence for those questions in a separate part of the file.

Organize the evidence around three different questions

Where can the dispute be heard? Preserve documents showing where the accident happened and where the vehicle was sold, resold, registered, and serviced. Record what is known about the manufacturer's business involving that model in the relevant state. Each defendant's contacts and role may require separate analysis.

What happened to this vehicle? Record the VIN, model, build date if available, component identifiers, repair history, and condition before and after the crash. Photos and inspection records can help track which tire, restraint, sensor, or other component was present at the time. Get guidance on preserving the vehicle and components before repair, dismantling, or disposal when a defect is at issue.

What supports the proposed defect explanation? A recall lookup is a starting point for identifying campaigns. NHTSA describes what its VIN search displays and what it can omit, including previously completed recalls. A campaign affecting a model may call for checking the precise VIN and component, repair history, and whether the concern matches the failure being investigated. NHTSA recall search and its coverage notes

A used-car receipt is part of the record

Imagine a driver buys a used car in State A and later moves to State B, where the manufacturer sells and services that model. If a crash happens in State B, a receipt from State A documents how the particular car got to the driver. Separate evidence about State B may show the connection between that market, the model, and the claimed injury. The analysis changes if the manufacturer had little relevant connection to the state or the underlying accident occurred elsewhere.

The value of the two Ford cases is the separation of these records. Where this car traveled, what the manufacturer did in the state, and what happened to this component are each factual questions. A single report is rarely the best source for all three.

Use the Evidence-to-Fact Map to attach one source to each entry in a vehicle history. Keep a distinct row for the alleged failure and for the evidence connecting it to the injury.

Keep the vehicle history beside the alleged component failure

Create one file for the chain of sale, registration, and service and another for evidence about the particular part. A service invoice can show where and when maintenance took place. A VIN lookup can identify some unrepaired recalls. Neither record alone identifies what caused a component to fail. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction ruling and NHTSA's recall search describe different questions. U.S. Supreme Court opinion in Ford · NHTSA recall lookup
