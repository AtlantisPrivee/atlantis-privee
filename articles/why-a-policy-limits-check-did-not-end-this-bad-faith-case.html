Why a Policy Limits Check Did Not End This Bad Faith Case

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

GEICO sent a $100,000 policy limits check nine days after a fatal Florida crash. The estate later sent it back. A jury awarded $8.47 million in the resulting wrongful death case. In the separate bad faith lawsuit that followed, the insurer's early check was one event in a longer record of what it had done to protect its insured from a judgment above his coverage. Florida Supreme Court opinion in Harvey

The Florida Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Harvey v. GEICO General Insurance Co. makes the timeline unusually easy to see. Read it beside Florida's current bad faith statute. The case explains why communication mattered to the historical verdict. The current statute supplies a tender rule with its own trigger and conditions. Florida Supreme Court opinion, pp. 2–7 · 2026 Florida Statutes, § 624.155

What went missing between the request and the reply

  • August 8, 2006. The crash happened. GEICO soon concluded that its insured, James Harvey, was at fault and recognized that the claim could exceed his policy limit.
  • August 14. A representative of the estate's lawyer asked the adjuster for a statement from Harvey about other insurance, assets, and whether he was acting for his business. The adjuster did not immediately pass that request to him.
  • August 17. GEICO tendered the full $100,000 with a release and a coverage affidavit.
  • August 31 and September 1. Harvey learned of the request. He told the adjuster he was arranging to review his financial information with counsel and asked how to convey that he was working on the response. The adjuster did not pass that update to the estate's lawyer despite a supervisor's instruction.
  • September 13. The estate returned the check and sued Harvey. The wrongful death verdict and excess judgment followed.

The Florida Supreme Court held that the record could support the bad faith jury's verdict in the later action brought by Harvey. It quashed the appellate decision and directed reinstatement of the judgment in the bad faith case. The court assessed the evidence under the standard for review of a directed verdict, with inferences drawn in favor of the party who had prevailed at trial. Harvey, pp. 2–8, 11–18

Tender and settlement are different entries on the timeline

A tender is the insurer putting a payment forward. A completed settlement resolves the claims described in the agreement. In Harvey, the chronology had evidence of the first event and a dispute about why the second never happened. That difference is easy to lose in a file labeled “limits paid.”

The estate's request also had a purpose. Its lawyer was evaluating whether another policy, the business, or other sources might matter to the decision about settling. The trial record included evidence about what the estate might have done if the information had been provided. The case is worth reading for that causal question. A missed message matters to the analysis when the evidence links it to a lost chance to resolve the claim and to the resulting exposure. Florida Supreme Court opinion in Harvey

The 90-day rule in current Florida law

Florida Statutes § 624.155(4)(a) provides a safe harbor for liability insurance bad faith claims, including those brought under common law. It applies when the insurer tenders the lesser of the policy limits and the amount demanded within 90 days after actual notice of a claim accompanied by sufficient evidence supporting the claimed amount. The facts about receipt, the supporting material, and the tender all matter to that provision.

Section 624.155(5) also states that negligence alone is insufficient for bad faith. It imposes a good faith duty on the insured, claimant, and their representatives in supplying information, setting deadlines, making demands, and attempting to settle. The factfinder may consider a failure to act in good faith when assessing damages. These provisions postdate the events in Harvey. The separate civil remedy notice provisions in subsection (3) answer a different procedural question. 2026 Florida Statutes, § 624.155(3)–(5)

What to write down when dates are disputed

Imagine an insurer acknowledges an accident report on April 1 and receives a documented demand packet on April 11. Record both dates and preserve the receipt evidence and contents of both communications. Which event supplied actual notice with sufficient supporting evidence can matter under subsection (4). The crash date and the date typed on the demand letter are useful, but neither records what reached the insurer by itself.

A useful claim chronology has one line per event with the sender, recipient, date sent, date received if known, requested action, supporting documents, and response. Mark the proposed release and the tender as separate records. Where a party disputes that an event occurred, attach the evidence for the competing dates. The Claim Timeline Builder can keep those sources alongside the entries.

Put proof of receipt beside the demand

Record the date a demand was sent, the date it reached the insurer, and which supporting records reached it at the same time. Keep the tender date and amount in a different row. This makes it possible to compare the actual evidence with Florida's liability bad-faith tender provision, which uses receipt of actual notice accompanied by sufficient supporting evidence. The terms of the offer and any release remain separate questions. Florida Statutes § 624.155
