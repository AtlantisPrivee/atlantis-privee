Can an Employer Health Plan Claim Your Injury Settlement?

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

The most important number in Robert Montanile's case changed as his settlement moved from one place to another. His recovery was $500,000. About $240,000 remained after payments to his attorneys and repayment of money they had advanced. The health plan had paid at least $121,044.02 for his initial medical care. It wanted repayment from the recovery.

By the time the plan sued, its claim depended on more than the amount it had paid. The U.S. Supreme Court asked whether the plan could identify settlement proceeds or property traceable to them, or whether it was trying to collect a debt from Montanile's general assets. That is the distinction at the heart of Montanile v. Board of Trustees, decided January 20, 2016. U.S. Supreme Court opinion, 577 U.S. 136, pp. 138–141

A recovery can lead to a second claim

Montanile was injured in a 2008 crash. His employer-related ERISA health plan paid for care under its benefit terms. The plan also had reimbursement language for recoveries from third parties, and Montanile signed a separate reimbursement agreement. After the settlement, the parties disputed the plan's claim. The attorney told the plan that money held for Montanile would be released unless the plan objected. The plan did not respond before the funds were distributed. It sued about six months later. Montanile, pp. 139–141

The plan invoked ERISA § 502(a)(3), a federal provision authorizing specified parties to seek equitable relief. Its reimbursement provision gave it a claim against an identified fund. Enforcing a lien against identifiable proceeds or property acquired with them is different from collecting money from assets unrelated to the fund. The Court held that the provision would not allow the plan to reach general assets if the whole fund had been dissipated on nontraceable items. It sent the case back to determine whether and how much of the settlement could still be traced. 29 U.S.C. § 1132(a)(3) · Montanile, pp. 142–151

Start with the plan document and the payment ledger

A letter calling an amount a lien leaves several questions open. Which plan made the payments? Who is asserting the right on its behalf? What language was in effect on the relevant dates? Which services and amounts make up the claim?

Ask for the operative reimbursement language, applicable amendments, and an itemized payment ledger. Compare the governing plan terms with any benefits summary and separate agreement you signed. Make a note of which document specifies the type of recovery covered, the share sought, and the treatment of legal fees or expenses. Keep the actual terms attached to the calculation.

For fee allocation, another Supreme Court case is useful. In US Airways, Inc. v. McCutchen, the Court held that equitable rules cannot override the terms of the plan in this kind of equitable-lien claim. Where that plan was silent about allocating attorney fees, the Court applied the common-fund rule to fill the gap. This gives a reader two different questions to ask when checking a demand. What does the plan say about the recovery? What does it say about the cost of obtaining it? US Airways v. McCutchen, 569 U.S. 88

Walk a settlement statement through two scenarios

Consider a hypothetical settlement of $100,000 with $30,000 in attorney fees and case costs, and a $25,000 reimbursement demand. Entering all three numbers produces $45,000 after those two deductions. The $45,000 tells you what follows if the demand is paid at its stated amount.

Now suppose the itemization includes $4,000 in services the parties are still reviewing. Keep the $25,000 written demand in the first scenario. The second can show $49,000 after a hypothetical reduction, with the $4,000 explicitly marked as disputed. This makes the unresolved issue visible without treating either result as the negotiated distribution.

The reimbursement ledger should use the amount the plan actually paid when it claims repayment of benefits. An initial provider charge, a plan payment, and a patient balance are different entries. Medical Bill–EOB Reconciler

Why the date of each letter belongs in the file

Log the first demand, responses, any dispute over itemization, and any agreement governing disbursement. Attach the settlement statement and correspondence showing where the funds were held. Resolve the reimbursement position before authorizing distribution with the people handling the claim. The litigation in Montanile concerned which remedy the plan could seek under a specific federal provision after money had moved.

This inquiry differs from Medicare recovery, Medicaid reimbursement, and provider liens. Identify the claimant and the source of its asserted right before applying a case or subtracting a figure from the settlement.
