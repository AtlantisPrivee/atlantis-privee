A $125,000 Crash Settlement and Medicare’s Repayment Demand

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

Vernon Hadden settled an injury claim for $125,000. Then he had to account for a different number. Medicare had paid $82,036.17 for his care and sought $62,338.07 back after reducing its recovery for a share of his attorney fees. The gap between those amounts is a window into what happens between Medicare's payment of a medical claim and its final recovery demand. Sixth Circuit opinion in Hadden

In Hadden v. United States, a divided Sixth Circuit upheld that demand in a dispute over a Kentucky accident. A second driver had caused the truck that struck Hadden to swerve and was never identified. Hadden argued that the truck's owner accounted for only a small share of the fault, and that the share should reduce Medicare's reimbursement. The majority treated the scope of the claim resolved by the settlement, which included his medical expenses, as decisive in this case. Sixth Circuit opinion, pp. 2–8

Why the number moves

Under the Medicare Secondary Payer framework, Medicare may make a conditional payment for care when a primary payer, such as a liability insurer, is expected to pay but cannot be expected to pay promptly. A later settlement, judgment, or other payment can trigger Medicare's right to recover eligible conditional payments. The payment history and the recovery demand are distinct records. CMS, Medicare's recovery process

In Hadden's case, the $125,000 bought a full release of his claims against the company that owned the truck. His suit had sought all his medical expenses. The majority rejected the proposed 10 percent allocation on those facts. The dissent would have read the reimbursement statute differently in light of what a compromise settlement represents. This is a useful case to read when someone uses a fault percentage to estimate the net check, because the majority tied the outcome to the claim and release in the record.

Match the document to its stage

A conditional payment letter is an inventory at a particular date. It lists charges Medicare's recovery contractor has associated with the incident and what Medicare has paid so far. An interim letter can be refreshed as more bills are paid. Review it for treatment connected to the accident and gather records for any line you dispute.

A settlement creates a new set of inputs. CMS asks for settlement information, including the amount and the fees and costs of obtaining it. Timing matters here because the agency can review payments through the date that applies under its process. Some eligible liability cases can use the Final Conditional Payment process through the Medicare Secondary Payer Recovery Portal before settlement. CMS explains the qualification and timing requirements separately. CMS, demand calculation options

A recovery demand states the amount CMS seeks. It comes with information about the recovery and the relevant rights or deadlines. Save the actual letter, rather than relying on a number copied into an earlier settlement worksheet. CMS, reimbursing Medicare

Follow the fees through one example

Imagine a case with an $80,000 settlement, $24,000 in eligible costs of obtaining the recovery, and $30,000 in related Medicare payments. When the applicable conditions for the proportional calculation under 42 C.F.R. § 411.37(c) are met, the eligible costs represent 30 percent of the recovery. That proportion of the Medicare payments is $9,000. Subtracting it gives a modeled Medicare recovery of $21,000.

The regulation provides a different calculation when Medicare's payments equal or exceed the judgment or settlement and also addresses other circumstances. Compare the actual demand to the applicable subsection. In the example, the $24,000 is already a settlement expense. The $9,000 is a reduction in the Medicare recovery, rather than an additional expense. Keeping the two entries separate prevents the same cost from being subtracted twice. 42 C.F.R. § 411.37

Check one disputed charge from beginning to end

Pick the largest line you cannot explain. Record the service date, provider, item identifier if available, and amount Medicare paid. Match those fields to the medical record or billing statement that shows what the service was for. Compare the record with the incident date and the treatment the settlement claim addressed. A same-day provider visit may include multiple services, so a match on provider and date alone can leave an open question.

Keep the gross settlement, acquisition costs, related conditional payments, disputed charges, and final demand in distinct fields. The Settlement Net Waterfall can help model those entries. For a private employer health plan's reimbursement claim, the plan documents and rules present a different inquiry from the Medicare recovery process.

Give every recovery figure a document and a date

If a file contains a conditional payment amount, a correction request, and a final demand, create a line for each. Include the issue date, what charges it covers, and whether you have a written update. CMS describes how conditional payments are reviewed and how a demand follows settlement reporting. The regulation addresses the calculation of Medicare recovery when procurement costs apply. CMS beneficiary recovery process · 42 C.F.R. § 411.37
