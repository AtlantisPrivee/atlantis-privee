When Private Social Media Becomes Injury Case Evidence

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱ 4 min read

A dispute over a riding accident reached New York's highest court with a question about Facebook messages. The defense wanted to know when a woman who described difficulty writing had sent messages and how long the messages were. The court had to decide how much of the account the defense could see. The difference between the time a message was sent and the time it took to write it is one reason the case still rewards a close reading.

In Forman v. Henkin, the New York Court of Appeals held that private social media can be discoverable when the requested material bears on the injuries and issues in the lawsuit. The order it reinstated had specific limits. The case offers a way to think about what a photo, timestamp, or post can establish, and which inferences require more evidence. New York Court of Appeals opinion, 30 NY3d 656

Why this account mattered

Kelly Forman alleged serious injuries after falling from a horse. She described changes in her social activities and trouble using a computer and composing messages. She testified that she had posted many photos reflecting her active life before the accident and had deactivated her account about six months afterward.

The defendant initially sought broad access to her private Facebook material. The trial court narrowed the request. It required the pre-accident photos of herself that she planned to use at trial, certain photos of herself that she posted after the accident, and authorization for records showing when she sent private messages after the accident and their length. The photo order excluded nudity and romantic encounters. The appellate court narrowed the order further. In 2018 the Court of Appeals reinstated the trial court's order. Forman, opinion at 659–661 and 665–667

The distinction between content and metadata is central. A message's timestamp and length disclose something about communication without necessarily revealing the words in the message. Their usefulness depends on the question they are meant to answer.

Relevance and scope come before privacy settings

In New York civil litigation, CPLR 3101 governs disclosure. In Forman, the court asked whether the particular incident and claimed injuries made relevant material likely to be found on the account. It then weighed the usefulness of the requested material against specific privacy concerns and tailored the scope of disclosure. The plaintiff's public profile did not have to contradict her testimony before relevant private material could be considered. New York CPLR 3101 · Forman, opinion at 662–666

The order depended on what Forman had put in dispute and what she said she posted. In another case, a request might identify a narrower period, a particular claimed physical activity, or a specific kind of post. The degree of detail can help a court weigh whether disclosure would answer a disputed question without drawing unrelated material into the case. New York's protective order provision offers a way to address concerns about the scope or use of disclosure. New York CPLR 3103

Read a timestamp with the right question in mind

Imagine two messages sent at 8:05 p.m. and 8:07 p.m. A record could show that two messages went out two minutes apart. It would take other evidence to establish when drafting began, whether text had been prepared earlier, or whether the person used dictation or assistance.

The same issue appears in photos. A picture posted after a crash may have been taken before it. A picture taken during a post-crash outing can show that someone attended, while leaving open how long they stayed or what help they needed. Compare the capture date, the posting date, the activity visible in the frame, and other dated records. Use captions and comments as context with their own author and timestamp.

Preserve the record and understand the request

Save original files, dates, captions, and relevant context when working with records you lawfully possess. Keep original copies intact if you create a review copy. In a pending or reasonably anticipated dispute, discuss preservation with counsel before changing or deleting relevant material.

The type of request matters. A question from an adjuster, a litigation discovery request, a request for a signed authorization, and a court order are different documents. Log the account, date range, kinds of material, recipient, and claimed basis for the request. Review any applicable order or protective restrictions before sharing private material.
