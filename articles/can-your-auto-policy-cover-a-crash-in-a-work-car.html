Can Your Auto Policy Cover a Crash in a Work Car?

✍️ Atlantis Editorial 📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱ 6 min read

A Pennsylvania detective was seriously injured when two drivers crashed into his unmarked police car. The other drivers' insurers and his employer's auto insurer paid their limits. He and his wife also paid for stacked underinsured motorist coverage on their personal vehicles. The question was whether those personal policies covered him in the city-owned car he regularly drove for work. Pennsylvania Supreme Court opinion in Rush

In Rush v. Erie Insurance Exchange, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that the regular-use exclusion in the policies at issue was consistent with the state's motor vehicle insurance statute as applied in the case. The outcome turns on a distinction easy to miss on a declarations page. A policy's limit tells you how much coverage is available for a covered claim. The definitions and exclusions determine whether that coverage applies to the trip in the first place. Pennsylvania Supreme Court opinion, pp. 1–4 and conclusion

The three layers that paid or declined

Matthew Rush was driving the City of Easton's Ford Fusion on November 28, 2015. The city's fleet policy provided $35,000 in underinsured motorist coverage. The household had three vehicles on two Erie policies, each with a $250,000 UIM limit per covered vehicle according to the court's description. They had elected stacked coverage. The city car was outside both personal policies' vehicle lists, and the parties agreed Rush used it regularly. Pennsylvania Supreme Court opinion in Rush

After the available payments from the other drivers and the city's insurer, the couple sought benefits from Erie. Erie relied on identical exclusions addressing regular use of a non-owned vehicle that the personal policy did not cover for UM or UIM. The trial court and Superior Court sided with the insureds on the statutory question. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court reversed. Rush, pp. 1–5

Underinsured motorist coverage and the regular-use clause

Underinsured motorist coverage, usually abbreviated UIM, addresses injuries for which an at-fault driver has insufficient liability coverage, subject to the governing policy and law. Pennsylvania requires insurers to offer UIM on the policies described in § 1731, and the purchase is optional. That statutory starting point and a purchased limit tell only part of the story. Pennsylvania Consolidated Statutes, 75 Pa.C.S. § 1731

In Rush, regular use and stacking addressed different questions. The exclusion concerned whether there was coverage for injury while using the particular non-owned vehicle. Stacking concerns how qualifying limits combine where applicable. You can read the stacking selection correctly and still have an unresolved question about the covered vehicle or trip.

An employer's fleet policy might name the business as the insured and extend particular coverage to drivers, occupants, or vehicles under its own terms. The household policy might use different definitions. Place the two contracts side by side. A reference to “you,” “resident,” “non-owned auto,” or “regular use” can point to a definition somewhere other than the page with the exclusion.

Reconstruct the coverage questions in order

  1. Identify the vehicle and date. Note the owner, VIN, trip purpose, and which policy versions were effective on the crash date. A renewal after the accident may contain different wording.
  2. Identify the available liability coverage. Log each carrier's coverage position and any payment or tender. Record whose policy it was and which claim it addressed.
  3. Get the employer or fleet policy wording. The declarations page and relevant coverage forms and endorsements answer different questions. Look for the UIM section and any conditions that apply to the driver or occupied vehicle.
  4. Read the personal policies for eligibility and exclusions. Save the exact page cited in a coverage letter and the definition it cross-references. Document how often the vehicle was available to the person and how it was actually used. The parties in Rush agreed regular use was present. A disputed-use case needs its own facts.
  5. Then examine limit and priority questions. Confirm the stacking election, any priority rules, payments already made, and how those matters fit the coverage that applies.

A useful contrast within the same work fleet

Imagine two employees injured in separate crashes in the same company car. One was assigned the vehicle most days and kept the keys. The other drove it once while a colleague was away. They can share the same fleet declarations page and have materially different evidence of their access and use. The policy language and governing law are the next facts to examine in each case.

Practical records can be simple. Keep the vehicle assignment schedule, a dated employer description of the arrangement, the fleet policy, the personal endorsements, and the insurer's letter together. A useful written question for a coverage review is, “Which policy version, provision, and facts about my use of the vehicle support your position?”

Does this apply outside Pennsylvania?

Rush interprets Pennsylvania's statute in the context of particular contracts and agreed facts. Work-vehicle claims in other states require a separate review of the policy and state law. In any jurisdiction, the file is easier to assess when it separates the use of the vehicle, the coverage trigger, the exclusion, and the size of the limit.

Keep responses from the fleet and personal insurers in one Claim Contact Ledger, using a separate entry for each policy.

Keep the policy clauses beside the vehicle assignment

In a coverage worksheet, give each policy its own row. Record who issued it, the effective dates, the insured vehicle or person, the UIM clause, and the exact exclusion the insurer invoked. Attach the vehicle assignment, trip purpose, and dates of use. A declaration showing the limit answers a different question from a clause defining whether the accident qualifies. That distinction is central to the Pennsylvania court's analysis. Pennsylvania Supreme Court opinion in Rush
